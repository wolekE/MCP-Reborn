Newton’s first & second laws

If the net external force is zero, velocity stays constant (at rest or moving in a straight line at constant speed). Otherwise ΣF = ma and the acceleration points along the net force. Inertia is resistance to a change in velocity: a property (mass), not a force.

First-law reasoning: what you know → what you can conclude

What you knowWhat you can conclude
Net force is zeroVelocity is constant, but it could be zero or nonzero.
Net force points rightAcceleration points right. The object could still be moving left (slowing down).
Moving left and slowing downAcceleration and net force point right.
Constant speed while turningVelocity changes direction, so acceleration is not zero.
Velocity is zero at one instantNet force may be nonzero (a tossed ball at its highest point).

Free-body diagrams: draw only forces acting on the chosen object (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied). Never draw “motion” or “inertia” as a force.

Newton’s third law

If A exerts a force on B, then B exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force on A: FA on B = −FB on A. Equal forces do not mean equal accelerations, because a = F/m.

Reminder: third-law pairs vs. balanced forces

THIRD-LAW PAIR
  • Act on two different objects
  • Equal size, opposite direction
  • Same interaction (same type of force), at the same time
  • Never cancel each other, because they act on different objects
BALANCED FORCES
  • Act on the same object
  • Vector sum is zero, so that object doesn’t accelerate
  • Usually different interactions (e.g. weight & normal)
  • Used to decide whether that one object accelerates
One forceIts third-law partner (swap “on” and “by”)
Earth pulls a book downwardThe book pulls Earth upward (toward the book)
Table pushes the book upwardThe book pushes the table downward
Swimmer pushes water backwardWater pushes the swimmer forward
Rocket pushes exhaust backwardExhaust pushes the rocket forward

Book on a table: weight (Earth on book) and normal force (table on book) balance, but they are not a third-law pair: both act on the book.

Truck vs. car: the forces are equal. The car’s bigger acceleration (and damage) comes from its smaller mass, not a bigger force.

Universal gravitation

Fg = G·m1·m2 / r², where r is the center-to-center distance. Gravity is always attractive, and each object feels the same size force (3rd law). Mass stays the same everywhere; weight changes with g.

Equilibrium

Translational equilibrium: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0, meaning at rest or at constant velocity. Routine: choose the object → draw its free-body diagram → choose axes → split angled forces into components → add signed components on each axis and solve.

Center of mass

The center of mass is the mass-weighted average position: xCM = Σ(m·x)/Σm. Pick one origin, use consistent units (kg & m or g & cm both work, since mass units cancel), and include only the objects in the system. For two masses it lies between them, closer to the heavier one.

Newton’s 3rd law

FA on B = −FB on A
aA = F/mA,  aB = F/mB
  • Pairs act on different objects, at the same time, same type of force.
  • Balanced forces act on the same object and sum to zero.
  • Swap test: partner of “A on B” is “B on A”.
  • Same force, smaller mass ⇒ bigger acceleration, opposite directions.

Center of mass

xCM = Σ(m·x) / Σm
yCM = Σ(m·y) / Σm
Move: ΔxCM = (mmoved/Mtotal)·Δxmoved
Add: xnew = xold + [madd/(Mold+madd)](xadd − xold)
  • Two masses: CM lies between them, closer to the heavier one.
  • Uniform symmetric object: CM is on every symmetry axis (one axis gives a line, not a point).
  • Uniform density ≠ midpoint for irregular shapes (bigger nail head ⇒ CM nearer the head).
  • Adding mass pulls the CM toward the added mass; how far depends on the mass.
  • Include a board only if it is part of the system and not negligible.
  • Mass units cancel: g & cm work fine; the answer keeps the position unit.
